What frontier models miss in Saudi law.

Basic questions reveal a knowledge gap. Realistic legal work needs a more demanding test.

Mohamed Zaghoo · Research note · November 2026

We asked four frontier models 79 practical Saudi legal questions in Arabic, without browsing. The strongest model fully answered 33 of 79—about 42%. None fully answered any of the ten professional-licensing questions.

A gap in practical knowledge

The models knew different parts of Saudi law. Gemini performed better in enforcement and family law; Astra performed better on court fees. Licensing was difficult for all four. This supports investment in better Arabic legal resources, but does not prove the information was absent from training data.

Context mattered too. Explicitly naming Saudi Arabia raised Gemini’s mean score from 0.468 to 0.591 and Muse’s from 0.144 to 0.326 in the reported runs.

Being wrong is not the same as abstaining

Astra abstained on 26 questions and was graded wrong on seven of its 53 attempts. Opus abstained on six and was graded wrong on 40 of 73 attempts. Legal evaluations should distinguish an admission of uncertainty from an incorrect amount or deadline stated as fact.

Recall is only the starting point

The next test should ask models to find the applicable source, identify exceptions, apply the right version of a rule, and produce a useful legal document. That requires realistic case files, expert-reviewed rubrics, and evaluation on unseen cases.

Arabic legal AI needs stronger knowledge resources and harder evaluations. A better score matters when it translates into better work.

Scope and limitations

This is a small, Saudi-only study with different answering configurations, one model grader, one answer sample per model, and potentially dated reference answers. It measures closed-book performance, not retrieval, legal drafting, or the effect of training. The results are not a controlled ranking of general model ability. Repeated runs with matched conditions and independent legal review are needed.

Source: Najiz Closed-Book evaluation: results, methodology, and reproducible scoring.
