With Saudi law named, the best of four frontier models fully answered 33 of 79 basic questions

We asked Gemini 4 Carbon, GPT-6 Astra, Claude Opus 5.5 and Muse Spark 1.3 79 short, practical questions about Saudi legal procedure, fees and licensing, from memory and without search. These are the kind of questions a court portal's help page answers. No model could reliably retrieve them, and none fully answered a single licensing question.

What we found

  1. Simple facts are the weak point. These questions ask for a fee, a deadline or a list of conditions that a Saudi lawyer would look up in seconds. The best model was fully correct on 42% of them, and two of the four on 14–15%.
  2. Licensing is a blind spot for every model. None of the four fully answered any of the 10 licensing questions (lawyers, notaries, foreign law firms, enforcement providers). The best mean score there was 0.13.
  3. The models know different parts of Saudi law. Gemini leads on criminal procedure (0.92), enforcement (0.72) and family law (0.71). Astra leads on court fees: it recalled 8 of the 16 fee and rate amounts exactly, against 6 for Gemini and 2 each for Opus and Muse.
  4. They fail in different ways. Astra says «لا أعرف» when unsure and was wrong on only 7 of the 53 questions it attempted. Opus attempted 73 and was graded wrong on 40, including 8 wrong amounts or deadlines stated as fact. Gemini attempted 64 and was wrong on 11.
  5. Naming the jurisdiction matters. The questions never say "Saudi Arabia". Without it, Gemini and Muse often said the country was unspecified or answered in general terms. One added line raised Gemini's mean score from 0.47 to 0.59 and Muse's from 0.14 to 0.33.

Where the gaps are

Licensing has the lowest category mean for all four models. Procedure categories generally perform better, while fee recall varies substantially. These author-assigned categories describe this question set; they do not establish which source documents a model encountered during training.

What the low scores establish—and what they do not

These are short factual questions, rather than end-to-end legal reasoning tasks. The results reveal a practical recall gap against the benchmark’s references: naming the jurisdiction did not make any model consistently correct. Arabic fluency alone is not evidence that a model can supply the right legal condition, amount or deadline.

Limited coverage of Saudi legal material in training is one possible explanation. Other explanations include outdated knowledge, difficulty retrieving learned facts, prompt sensitivity and imperfections in the reference answers. This study does not inspect training corpora, isolate the effect of Arabic, or establish the cause of the gap.

The uneven strengths nevertheless give data builders a concrete agenda: preserve jurisdiction, source provenance and effective dates; represent exceptions explicitly; and evaluate precise amounts and periods separately from plausible prose. Whether additional data improves a model must then be tested on unseen material.

Recall and reliability are different measurements. An abstention receives a low benchmark score, but it is not the same failure as an incorrect answer presented with certainty. Report both coverage and errors among attempted answers.

How the models were run

All four answered the same 79 questions in Arabic, closed-book, with the same core instructions: no browsing, tools or files; do not invent laws, amounts, article numbers or citations; say «لا أعرف» if unsure; at most 150 words. How each model learned that the questions concern Saudi law differed:

How we graded

We broke each reference answer into 270 required elements in total and weighted them by type:

ElementWeight
Core principle3
Deadline, period or term2
Amount, rate or payment2
Condition, exception or detail1
One item in an enumerated list1

An answer that contradicts the reference scores 0. A wrong amount or timeline multiplies the score by 0.3. A wrong citation costs 2 points. Correct means a score of at least 0.85 with every core principle, deadline and amount present; partial means at least 0.40.

Claude Sonnet 5.5 graded all four answer sets one question at a time, at temperature 0. For each question it saw the reference, the rubric elements and the four answers labelled A–D in a fresh random order; the key never reached it. We recomputed all 316 scores from its element-by-element calls and found no arithmetic errors.

Limitations

  • Saudi context was not delivered identically. Gemini and Muse received one explicit instruction; Astra and Opus had implicit context of different strength. The answering setups also differed.
  • One grader. Sonnet 5.5 is from the same family as Opus 5.5. Shared model lineage is a potential source of bias; its direction and size have not been established. No lawyer has reviewed the grader’s close calls.
  • Grading noise. Astra's and Opus's unchanged answers were graded in two separate runs; their mean scores moved by up to 0.03. This observed variation cautions against interpreting small score differences; it is not a statistical significance threshold.
  • One sample per model. Answers vary between runs, so a single run cannot separate ability from chance.
  • Small and Saudi-only. 79 questions, mostly procedure and fees, from one portal. The results do not cover other Arab jurisdictions or legal drafting.
  • Reference answers are the standard. A few reference answers do not directly answer their question, and some may be out of date.
  • Possible contamination. The benchmark has been public since 2024 and may be in any model's training data.
  • Closed-book only. The effect of supplying context passages has not been measured in these results. This run evaluates recall under the stated conditions, not source retrieval or reading supplied evidence.

Why Arabic legal AI needs more complex evaluations

Getting a short answer right is necessary for some workflows, but insufficient for legal work. A system can recall a fee and still choose the wrong procedure, miss a material exception, rely on a superseded rule or draft an unsupported conclusion. Conversely, a model that abstains from memory may perform well when it can consult authoritative sources. Neither outcome is measured here.

A next-stage benchmark should use realistic, expert-reviewed case files and test a sequence of decisions with observable outputs:

  1. Establish the case. Extract parties, dates, claims and missing facts from Arabic documents. Ask a targeted clarification when the record is insufficient.
  2. Find the applicable authority. Retrieve the relevant rule, check jurisdiction and effective date, and distinguish primary authority from commentary or an obsolete version.
  3. Apply the rule. Connect evidence to each legal condition, handle exceptions, and calculate amounts or deadlines with explicit assumptions.
  4. Produce a usable work product. Draft a memorandum, procedural checklist or filing supported by traceable citations. Distinguish established facts from allegations and unresolved questions.
  5. Respond to changed evidence. Revise the recommendation when a date, document or jurisdiction changes, without retaining conclusions that no longer follow.

The scoring should examine both the final answer and the observable work that produced it: retrieved sources, calculations, intermediate artifacts and revisions. A polished memo should not compensate for a decisive error in the applicable law. Expert rubrics should identify critical failures as well as partial credit.

Compare closed-book, supplied-context and retrieval-enabled settings separately. Use repeated runs under matched conditions, independent legal review and a grader checked against human judgments. Hold out entire cases and closely related variants, rather than letting steps from the same case appear in both training and evaluation.

The target is transfer to unfamiliar legal work. A score increase on exposed questions is not enough. Better Arabic legal data should improve performance on held-out cases, with fewer unsupported claims and more appropriate requests for evidence.

These are proposed evaluation requirements, not capabilities or training improvements demonstrated by the 79-question experiment. Our Arabic legal reward-reliability study addresses the complementary problem: whether the evaluator itself rewards the right behavior.

Next

We plan to run the same questions with the benchmark's context passages, take several samples per model with identical instructions and settings, add a second grader from a different model family, and have a Saudi lawyer review the disputed grades.
